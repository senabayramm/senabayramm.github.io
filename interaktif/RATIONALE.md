The Shrinking Universe is an interactive web experiment investigating the paradox between absolute freedom of action and systemic spatial collapse. The participant navigates a digital avatar across a confined canvas, yet every single navigation input permanently contracts the boundaries of the environment, turning unconstrained agency into a self-imposed enclosure.

We combined Agency and Possibility to construct a dependent tension. Agency lives in the unhindered movement mechanics, allowing the participant to direct the avatar freely via pointer interactions or keyboard controls. Possibility lives in the spatial restriction rule: each discrete movement dynamically reduces the width and height of the container. Rather than functioning as separate features, they directly sabotage one another; the more you exercise your agency to explore, the faster you exhaust the space of possibilities provided by the system.

Two theoretical frameworks shaped our design decisions. First, Norman’s (1988) principles of design affordances guided how the interface communicates actionable paths through labeled buttons and intuitive keyboard mappings, ensuring immediate usability without verbal instructions. Second, Salen and Zimmerman’s (2004) theory of the "magic circle" and formal game rules inspired the architectural constraint where systemic limits transform a simple navigation task into a tense, finite tragedy of spatial exhaustion. A notable counterpoint arose during user testing: excessive shrinkage occasionally caused disorientation, demonstrating the fine line between engaging systemic pressure and frustrating user experience.

Through prototype testing, we observed that participants initially reveled in their movement freedom, completely ignoring the shrinking borders until movement became impossible. This behavioral shift led us to refine our spatial decay thresholds, confirming that spatial feedback fundamentally alters how users value their remaining choices.
